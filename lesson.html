Reading a Band 9 Discussion Essay

A walk-through for one student session: the five question types, how a Band 9 introduction is built, and a full paragraph-by-paragraph breakdown of the model essay — with every B2+ word explained.

Part 1

The five IELTS Task 2 question types

Every Task 2 prompt gives a topic and a specific instruction. Almost all prompts fall into one of these five categories.

1. Opinion — "To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

Take a clear position (strongly agree, strongly disagree, or partially agree) and support it throughout the essay.

2. Discussion — "Discuss both views and give your opinion."

Analyse both sides objectively, giving equal weight to each, while still stating your own stance clearly.

3. Direct / double question — two related questions in one prompt.

Answer both questions directly, usually in separate body paragraphs (e.g. Why is this happening? What solutions exist?).

4. Advantages vs. disadvantages

Evaluate the benefits and drawbacks of a phenomenon, and state whether the advantages outweigh the disadvantages.

5. Problem & solution / cause & effect

Explain the reasons or problems behind a situation and propose realistic solutions, or explain its effects.

Part 2

The model essay, at a glance

Prompt: "Increasing travel between countries enables people to learn different cultures, or does it increase tension between people from different countries?"

Category: Discussion / direct-choice essay — weighing cultural learning against cultural tension.

Stance: The writer rejects the idea that travel causes lasting conflict, arguing instead that it builds mutual understanding and economic cooperation.

Structure

  • Introduction — sets the context and states the thesis, rejecting the negative view.
  • Body 1 — economic drivers that encourage mutual respect between locals and visitors.
  • Body 2 — how communication bridges cultural gaps over time.
  • Body 3 (counter-argument) — admits small, accidental frictions at tourist spots, then shows how exposure resolves them.
  • Conclusion — reaffirms that travel improves understanding rather than hostility.
Part 3

The universal introduction formula

A Band 7+ introduction needs only 2–3 sentences. This formula works for every essay type:

Introduction = Hook / Context + Paraphrased Topic + Thesis Statement
1. Background→2. Paraphrase the prompt→3. State your position
Part 4

Deconstructing the model introduction

1 · Background / hook sentence

"Globalisation is a catch-all term that refers to any activity that involves more than one country, for example, travels from one country to another."

Purpose: introduces the general theme (globalisation/travel) to set the context immediately.

Keep this brief, or merge it with the paraphrase, so more time is left for the body paragraphs.

2 · Paraphrasing the prompt

"The dramatic increase in transnational travel in recent years has sparked controversy over the potential impacts of this trend on individual countries, especially those new member states of globalisation. Some people are concerned that the upsurge in new arrivals will prompt local hostility against visitors, instead of promoting their understanding of mutual cultural background."

Purpose: restates the exam prompt using synonyms and different grammar.

increasing travels → dramatic increase in transnational travel / upsurge in new arrivals
increase tension → prompt local hostility
learn different cultures → promoting their understanding on mutual cultural background

3 · Thesis statement

"This notion should be rejected as one can see many facts in favour of this development between countries."

Purpose: answers the prompt directly and tells the reader the writer's exact position.

Never omit the thesis. Examiners look for a clear stance from the start to award Band 7+ for Task Achievement.

Part 5

A simple template for your student

Share this two-sentence skeleton during the lesson — it works for building any introduction:

It is often argued that [Side A], while others believe that [Side B].

In my opinion, [your clear position], and this essay will discuss the main reasons supporting this view.
Part 6

The full essay, paragraph by paragraph

Introduction
Globalisation is a catch-all term that refers to any activity that involves more than one country, for example, travels from one country to another. The dramatic increase in transnational travel in recent years has sparked controversy over the potential impacts of this trend on individual countries, especially those new member states of globalisation. Some people are concerned that the upsurge in new arrivals will prompt local hostility against visitors, instead of promoting their understanding on mutual cultural background. This notion should be rejected as one can see many facts in favour of this development between countries.
Sets the scene, paraphrases the prompt, and states the thesis: travel does not cause lasting hostility.
Body 1 — economic drivers
The first reason why international travel would never bring conflict is rooted in the fact that both visitors and locals are economically motivated. International travel opens up opportunities for business development throughout the world. Entrepreneurs are interested not only in the domestic market but also in the overseas market. Foreigners should respect the culture of a country before winning over the local people. In turn, locals should show hospitality to visitors in exchange for their trust. They share a view that acceptance of each other's cultural background is a necessary condition for cooperation.
Shared economic interest gives both sides a reason to respect one another, which builds trust rather than resentment.
Body 2 — communication bridges the gap
Understanding a culture has other implications. Differences in social background, cultural values and religious belief might make the discrepancy of foreigners and local inhabitants on some issues indelible; however, the higher the level of communication and understanding. Arabians, for example, used to consider westerners as their foes. Now they have concrete relations with their western allies in many fields. In the initial stage, their divergence seemed inherent but over time, with better mutual understanding, they take the same position on many issues.
Uses a real-world example (Arab–Western relations) to show that early cultural distance narrows as contact and communication increase.
Body 3 — counter-argument and refutation
Undeniably, it is likely that in some resorts, foreign visitors repel the local community with their scant regard for the environment and conventions when they first arrive. However, it should be noted that most of this is incidental, rather than intentional. Instead, visitors disobey rules and conventions simply because they have no knowledge of them. This situation is expected to be improved with the passing of time, when visitors from different countries increase their knowledge of a local culture.
Acknowledges the opposing view honestly, then reframes early friction as a knowledge gap that education and time resolve — not real hostility.
Conclusion
According to the above analysis, we can observe that the increase in international travel should not be taken as the cause of any conflict that arises between two countries. Alternatively, one should recognise its role in improving mutual understanding between two countries.
Restates the thesis and closes the loop between the two body arguments and the counter-argument.
Part 7

Vocabulary bank — B2+ words from the essay

Each word: a plain definition, plus two example sentences in fresh contexts.

catch-all
a broad term covering many different things at once.
"Wellness" has become a catch-all word for anything to do with health.
The report used "infrastructure" as a catch-all for roads, power and water systems.
to spark (something)
to suddenly cause a reaction, discussion, or event to begin.
The new tax proposal sparked heated debate in parliament.
A single photo sparked a nationwide conversation about plastic waste.
impact (n.)
a strong effect or influence that something has on a situation.
Researchers are studying the impact of screen time on children's sleep.
The factory closure had a major impact on the local economy.
hostility
unfriendly or aggressive behaviour or feeling toward someone.
The manager's harsh feedback created hostility within the team.
Years of hostility between the two neighbours ended with a simple apology.
rooted in
based on or originating from something, often deeply.
Her confidence is rooted in years of consistent practice.
The conflict is rooted in a land dispute from decades ago.
entrepreneur
a person who starts and runs a business, usually accepting financial risk.
The young entrepreneur launched her first company at nineteen.
Local entrepreneurs opened three new cafés in the district this year.
discrepancy
a noticeable difference between two things that should match.
There's a discrepancy between the sales figures and the bank statement.
Teachers noticed a discrepancy between his test scores and his classwork.
indelible
impossible to remove, forget, or change.
The trip left an indelible memory of the mountains at sunrise.
Her first novel made an indelible mark on modern literature.
foe
an enemy or opponent.
The two companies, once bitter foes, agreed to merge.
In the story, the knight finally faces his oldest foe.
concrete (adj.)
specific and definite, not vague or abstract.
The manager asked for a concrete plan, not just general ideas.
Scientists need concrete evidence before publishing their findings.
resort (n.)
a place people visit for holidays and relaxation.
The beach resort was fully booked for the summer season.
They spent the honeymoon at a quiet mountain resort.
to repel
to push away or cause someone to feel dislike or distance.
The strong smell of the spray repels mosquitoes effectively.
His arrogant tone repelled most of the people he met.
scant
very little, barely enough, or insufficient.
The article received scant attention when it was first published.
With scant resources, the small team still finished the project on time.
incidental
happening by chance as a minor part of something, not planned.
The noise from the street was incidental to the main recording.
Any mistakes in the draft were incidental, not deliberate.
to disobey
to fail to follow a rule, instruction, or authority.
The driver was fined for disobeying the traffic signal.
Few students dared to disobey the strict headteacher.
